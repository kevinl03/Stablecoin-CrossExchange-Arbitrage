\subsection{Research Questions and Problem Statement}

Cross-exchange stablecoin arbitrage exploits price differences for the same asset across centralized exchanges. While the idea appears straightforward—buy on the cheaper exchange and sell on the more expensive one—practical execution is significantly more complex. Profitable opportunities depend not only on visible price spreads, but also on fees, slippage, transfer delays, liquidity constraints, exchange reliability, and the short time windows in which arbitrage remains viable. Manually identifying profitable multi-exchange trading cycles is slow, error-prone, and not scalable.

This project formulates cross-exchange stablecoin arbitrage as a graph-planning problem and develops an automated route planner that computes complete, executable trade-and-transfer cycles rather than simple pairwise price gaps. By incorporating trading fees, withdrawal costs, transfer latencies, liquidity depth, and live market data via CCXT, the system generates execution-ready arbitrage paths. Unlike prior tools such as Crypto Advisor (Oantă \& Coroiu, 2023), which detect price discrepancies without modeling full execution cycles or risk, our approach integrates real-world constraints and applies A* search with domain-specific heuristics.

Our methodology is structured around several research questions. First, do certain heuristics consistently outperform others on specific classes of trading instances? We examine whether $h_4$ (chain congestion + exchange reliability) dominates due to richer risk modeling, whether $h_1$ (liquidity) performs best in high-volume markets but struggles in sparse ones, whether $h_2$ (slippage) excels when order-book structure is the primary bottleneck, and whether $h_3$ (parallel multi-start) can discover profitable opportunities missed by single-start A*.

Second, how does each heuristic influence A*’s node-expansion behavior? Because heuristics determine search order, understanding expansion bias is critical. For example, $h_1$ prioritizes deep markets, potentially skipping thin but profitable routes. $h_2$ penalizes shallow order books and favors conservative execution paths. $h_4$ deprioritizes fast-but-risky blockchains or unreliable exchanges, trading theoretical profit for robustness. In contrast, $h_3$ diversifies exploration by expanding multiple regions in parallel.

Third, how does algorithmic performance change as structural difficulty increases? Hard instances arise from liquidity sparsity, limited chain overlap, high withdrawal fees (e.g., Ethereum), short arbitrage windows, and fragmented exchange listings. These constraints produce complex search landscapes analogous to bottleneck-heavy planning problems.

Finally, when heuristics return suboptimal routes, how close are they to the best-known solution? We compare achieved profit against the maximum observed across all heuristics, assess whether certain heuristics consistently produce near-optimal results, evaluate whether Weighted A*’s safety bias reduces profitability, and determine whether parallel multi-start mitigates weaknesses of single-heuristic searches. We also identify failure patterns where certain heuristics degrade under increased order size or path depth.

\subsection{Why Cross-Exchange Stablecoin Arbitrage Matters}

Stablecoins serve as the primary liquidity bridges in cryptocurrency markets, connecting exchanges, blockchains, and trading pairs. Because centralized exchanges operate independently, price discrepancies for the same USD-pegged asset frequently arise due to fragmented liquidity, withdrawal delays, blockchain congestion, regional demand asymmetries, and operational frictions. These deviations are typically small in magnitude but persistent in structure, forming a dense and highly connected cross-exchange network.

Unlike volatile cryptocurrencies, stablecoins operate within a bounded price band anchored to fiat currency. This bounded deviation property reframes arbitrage from speculative price prediction into a constrained execution-optimization problem. Profitability is determined not by directional market movement, but by the ability to coordinate trades and transfers while minimizing fees, slippage, latency, and risk. This creates a uniquely structured planning domain in which path-dependent execution costs dominate expected return.

During periods of macroeconomic instability or crypto-market stress, stablecoin flows intensify as capital migrates toward dollar-pegged assets. These periods amplify temporary cross-exchange imbalances, increasing both spread frequency and execution complexity. As a result, the structured stablecoin arbitrage graph becomes particularly valuable during economic volatility, where efficient routing of dollar-denominated liquidity provides both profitability opportunities and market-stabilizing effects. The dataset therefore captures economically meaningful liquidity movements rather than transient speculative signals.


\subsection{Key Challenges (Liquidity, Slippage, Latency, Reliability)}

Cross-exchange arbitrage is constrained by several real-world frictions that significantly complicate route planning.

\textbf{Liquidity.} Order-book depth determines how much capital can be deployed without moving the market. Thin markets may display attractive spreads but cannot support meaningful trade volume without eroding profit.

\textbf{Slippage.} Execution prices depend on order-book structure. Large orders incur nonlinear price impact, meaning theoretical profit often differs from realized profit.

\textbf{Latency.} Cross-exchange transfers require blockchain confirmations, and arbitrage windows may close before assets arrive. Chain congestion and confirmation delays introduce time-dependent risk.

\textbf{Reliability.} Exchanges may experience withdrawal suspensions, API instability, or execution failures. Some blockchains offer faster transfers but higher operational risk.

These constraints interact in complex ways. For example, faster chains may reduce latency but carry higher reliability risk, while deep liquidity markets may impose higher trading fees. Effective arbitrage planning therefore requires balancing profit maximization with robustness under uncertainty. Our heuristic-guided A* framework explicitly models these trade-offs to prioritize routes that are not only theoretically profitable but realistically executable.
